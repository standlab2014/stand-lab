Research

The questions we ask

Alzheimer's disease begins two decades before the first symptom. Amyloid gathers, tau spreads through connected brain regions, and cognition holds steady — until it does not. Our work is about reading that silent interval: who is already on the path, how fast they are moving along it, and what can still be changed.

We work at the point where imaging, blood tests and the clinic meet. A tau PET scan can now stage the disease in a living person; a blood draw can flag the same pathology at a fraction of the cost. Neither is useful until we know exactly what its numbers mean for the patient in front of us.

Our work takes four forms:

  • Research — biomarker studies in deeply characterised cohorts, from PET imaging and plasma assays through to nationwide population data.
  • Clinical care — a memory clinic for early-onset dementia, mild cognitive impairment and subjective cognitive decline, including counselling on new disease-modifying therapies.
  • Collaboration — international consortia including LEADS and multicentre tau PET tracer comparisons, alongside partners at UCSF and across Korea.
  • Teaching — training residents, fellows and graduate students, and serving on the education and academic committees of the Korean Dementia Association.
01

Tau and amyloid PET

Staging Alzheimer's pathology in vivo: how tau PET predicts who converts from mild cognitive impairment to dementia, how visual reads compare with quantification, and how different tracers agree across centres.

02

Blood-based biomarkers

Plasma p-tau217 and related markers as a scalable first line — detecting Alzheimer neuropathology across clinical syndromes, and predicting the pathological stage that PET would show.

03

Risk, prevention and brain health

What moves the curve: physical activity, sleep, blood pressure, diabetes and metabolic health, studied in both biomarker cohorts and nationwide population data covering millions of people.

04

How tau spreads

Network models of tau propagation, the interaction between amyloid and tau that accelerates it, and how the pattern differs between early- and late-onset disease.

Team

The people behind the work

Hanna Cho, M.D., Ph.D.

Principal Investigator

Professor of Neurology at Gangnam Severance Hospital, Yonsei University College of Medicine, where she leads the Seoul AD Biomarker Team. She trained in medicine at Yonsei University and completed her M.S. and Ph.D. at its Graduate School of Medicine, followed by a neurology fellowship at Samsung Medical Center. She was a visiting scholar at the UCSF Memory and Aging Center and studied Equity in Brain Health at the Global Brain Health Institute, UCSF.

Her research centres on tau and amyloid PET imaging and blood-based biomarkers of Alzheimer's disease. She serves as Director of Research for the Korean Dementia Association and sits on the Scientific Program Committee of the Alzheimer's Association International Conference.

About

A compass for those facing dementia

The lab is based at the Future Medical Research Center of Gangnam Severance Hospital, in the Department of Neurology at Yonsei University College of Medicine. It grew out of a memory clinic — out of the gap between what a scan could show and what a patient could be told.

That gap is closing. Tau PET can now stage the disease in a living person, and a blood test can flag the same pathology without a scanner. Our work is to make those readings dependable enough to act on: to say who is at risk, how quickly the disease is likely to move, and which of that is still open to change.
